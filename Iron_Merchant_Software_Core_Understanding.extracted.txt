IRON MERCHANTMANAGEMENT SYSTEMCore Understanding & Business LogicProduct vision • Inventory model • Cutting • Sales • Payments • Accounting • Database • SecurityPrepared as the single source of truth for software design and implementationDocument purposeThis document explains what the Iron Merchant Management System is, how the business operates, which rules the software must enforce, and how the main modules fit together. It is intended for the business owner, product designer, developer, database designer, and anyone implementing or maintaining the system. It records the current agreed understanding; it is not a claim that a complete production database migration or tested application already exists.Important  Treat the rules in this document as business requirements. If a screen, report, API, or database operation conflicts with them, the business rules take priority.1. Product overviewThe software is a private, single-business management system for an iron/steel merchant. Its main job is to maintain accurate physical stock, calculate inventory cost, record purchases and sales, manage cutting and scrap, track customer and supplier balances, record payments and expenses, and provide reliable financial and stock reports.1.1 Operating modelOne business owner/admin uses the system. There is no employee login or employee-management module in the current scope.The system is an internal management tool, not an e-commerce website.There is no customer-facing online ordering, shopping cart, or public product catalogue.The business currency is Pakistani Rupees (PKR).The business sells iron shafts as full lengths or as pieces created by cutting a shaft.Both full-shaft and cut-piece sales are priced per kilogram, but they use separate selling rates.Scrap produced by cutting is tracked and sold separately from usable shaft stock.1.2 Main outcomesKnow exactly which physical pieces are in stock, including their measured length and weight.Know the value of usable shaft stock using weighted average cost (WAC).Prevent the same shaft or cut piece from being sold or consumed twice.Track cutting outputs, scrap, and any unexplained cutting variance.Generate invoices and track what each customer owes.Track supplier purchases and what the business owes suppliers.Record partial payments and allocate them to invoices.Keep a complete, auditable history instead of deleting confirmed transactions.Produce stock, sales, purchase, profit, receivable, payable, and expense reports.2. Core business concepts3.1 Product vs physical inventory pieceA product represents a sellable iron specification, not one physical shaft. For example, a product may describe a particular diameter, grade/material, and standard length. Multiple physical shafts can belong to the same product.Every physical shaft or cut piece must have its own inventory record and unique piece_code. This is essential because actual weights can differ even when pieces have the same product specification.Product: the specification used for grouping, pricing, and weighted-average costing.Inventory piece: one real, identifiable piece of iron with its own measured length, measured weight, status, and cost value.Cutting lineage: links a source shaft to the pieces created from it.Scrap lot: a separately tracked quantity created during cutting; it is not usable shaft inventory.3.2 Measurements and moneyLength is recorded in feet (ft).Weight is recorded in kilograms (kg).Selling rates are stored as PKR per kg.Money and weights must use appropriate fixed-precision numeric database types, not floating-point types.Store the actual measured length and weight for every received shaft and every cutting output.Keep the original measured values and transaction snapshots; do not silently overwrite historical measurements.4. Inventory rules4.1 Individual shaft recordsEach received shaft should have at least the following information:Unique piece code / barcode-friendly identifierProduct/specification referenceActual measured length in feetActual measured weight in kilogramsCurrent carrying cost/valueCurrent statusPurchase item/source referenceParent piece reference when it was created by cuttingSource cutting record referenceCreated/updated timestamps and owner reference4.2 Inventory statusesStatusMeaningfullA complete shaft available for sale or cutting.cutA child piece created by cutting and available for sale or further permitted processing.reservedTemporarily held for a future operation; must not be sold or consumed by another operation.splitA source piece that has been consumed by a cutting operation. It remains in history, not available stock.soldA piece that has been sold and is no longer available.scrappedA piece/record that is no longer usable as shaft stock; scrap itself is tracked in scrap lots.4.3 Stock balance and cost balanceThe system should maintain product-level aggregate balances for efficient reporting, but individual physical piece records remain the operational source of truth. Aggregate balances must only be changed through validated database transactions/RPCs, never by direct frontend edits.Product stock balance: total available shaft kilograms and other relevant stock quantities by product.Product cost balance: total carrying value of sellable shaft stock by product.Scrap balance: maintained separately from sellable shaft stock.Reconciliation: regularly compare aggregate balances with the sum of eligible physical piece records and their values.5. Purchase and receiving workflowA purchase records the supplier, date, reference/document number, product(s), purchase costs, additional landed costs, and the individual shafts received. Receiving must capture each shaft separately rather than recording only one total quantity.5.1 Receiving stepsCreate or select the supplier.Create a purchase document in draft state.Select the product specification.Enter the measured length and measured weight for every physical shaft received.Enter purchase cost and additional landed costs such as transport or handling.Allocate landed costs to the product/received stock using a documented allocation rule (weight-based by default).Confirm the purchase: create inventory-piece records, stock movements, supplier payable/payment records as applicable, and accounting journal entries.Recalculate the product's weighted average cost.5.2 Weighted average cost (WAC)Usable shaft inventory uses weighted average cost per kilogram, calculated separately for each product. The purchase cost includes allocated landed costs.WAC formula  New WAC = (Existing shaft inventory value + New landed purchase cost) ÷ (Existing shaft kg + Newly received shaft kg).Example: if a product has 500 kg with a carrying value of PKR 100,000 and a new purchase adds 300 kg at a landed cost of PKR 66,000, the new WAC is PKR 166,000 ÷ 800 kg = PKR 207.50/kg.Use a consistent precision policy. If per-piece values are rounded, allocate any rounding remainder deterministically (for example, to the final received piece) so the sum of piece values equals the purchase value exactly.6. Cutting and scrap managementCutting is an inventory transformation, not a sale and not a source of profit. One source shaft is consumed and one or more child pieces are created. Any scrap is recorded as a separate scrap lot.6.1 Cutting workflowSelect an available source shaft and lock it during the operation.Record the cutting operation and its date/reference.Enter each output piece's actual measured length and actual measured weight.Enter scrap weight/quantity when scrap is produced.Record any unexplained weight/length variance rather than silently discarding it.Mark the source shaft as split so it cannot be sold or cut again.Create child inventory pieces linked to the source and cutting record.Create a separate scrap lot for the scrap output.Update stock and cost balances atomically, then write stock movements and accounting journals.6.2 Cost allocation during cuttingThe source shaft's carrying cost must be allocated across the usable child pieces and scrap according to their measured output weights. Cutting must not create revenue or profit. The combined value assigned to usable outputs and scrap, plus any recognized cutting-loss expense, must reconcile to the source carrying value.Critical accounting rule  Scrap is not included in the denominator or balance used to calculate sellable shaft WAC. It has its own quantity and valuation.6.3 Cutting varianceThe system must compare source weight with total child-piece weight plus scrap weight. Any difference must be captured as a variance. If the difference represents an actual loss, the corresponding cost is posted to a cutting-loss expense account. Never allow the system to silently make inventory value disappear.6.4 Partial-length saleA customer requesting a partial length should not cause the system to reduce the length/weight of a source shaft informally. First perform a cutting operation that creates a child piece with its own measured length and weight, then sell that child piece. Ideally, the cut-and-sale workflow is one atomic database operation so a failed sale does not leave an unintended split.6.5 Reversing a cutting operationA confirmed cutting operation must not be deleted. It may only be reversed through a controlled reversal workflow. Reversal is allowed only if all output pieces and the scrap lot are still unused, unsold, uncut, and otherwise available. If any output has been consumed, the system must block a simple reversal and require an explicit corrective process.7. Sales and invoicing7.1 Sale types and ratesSale typeRuleFull shaft saleA full physical shaft is sold; use the full-shaft selling rate per kg.Cut piece saleA child piece created through cutting is sold; use the cut-piece selling rate per kg.Scrap saleA scrap lot is sold; use a separate scrap rate or an explicitly entered and snapshotted rate.7.2 Sale amountSales formula  Line amount = Actual sold weight (kg) × Applicable selling rate (PKR/kg).The invoice line must store a snapshot of the applied rate, measured/sold weight, and calculated amount. Changing today's product price must never change an old invoice.7.3 Sales rulesOnly available inventory pieces or available scrap lots can be sold.A physical piece can be sold only once; stock consumption and invoice creation must be atomic.Full and cut sales use different price categories/rates.Scrap sales use scrap pricing, not the cut-piece rate.A partial-length request is handled by cutting first, then selling the resulting piece.The sale records customer, invoice date/number, line items, weights, rate snapshots, discounts if permitted, and total.A confirmed sale is reversed by a credit note/reversal transaction, not by deleting the invoice.8. Payments, customer accounts and supplier accountsInvoices and payments are separate records. This is necessary because a customer may pay in multiple installments, pay several invoices together, or pay an advance before an invoice exists.8.1 Payment allocationA payment records direction (money received or money paid), party, date, amount, method, reference and notes.A payment allocation links a payment to one or more invoices/bills.A single invoice may receive multiple partial payments.A single payment may be allocated across multiple invoices.The system must validate that allocations do not exceed the payment amount or the outstanding amount on the target document, except where an explicitly supported credit/advance workflow applies.Unallocated amounts remain as customer/supplier advances or unapplied credits; they must not be incorrectly treated as payment against a specific invoice.Outstanding balances must be calculated from posted documents and allocations, not from a manually editable balance field.8.2 Customer and supplier ledgersCustomer accounts show invoices, receipts, credit notes, allocations, advances, and the resulting receivable. Supplier accounts show purchases/bills, payments, debit/credit adjustments, allocations, and the resulting payable.A simple formula such as total sales minus all receipts is insufficient when payments can be unallocated, allocated to multiple invoices, reversed, or adjusted by credit notes. Use the allocation ledger and posted accounting entries as the reliable basis.9. Expenses and financial accounting9.1 Double-entry accountingThe system should use double-entry bookkeeping. Each posted journal entry must have total debits equal to total credits. Business events create journal entries in the same database transaction as their operational records, so inventory, invoices, balances, and accounting cannot drift apart.Business eventIllustrative journal treatmentCredit saleDebit Accounts Receivable; Credit Sales Revenue.Cost of goods soldDebit Cost of Goods Sold; Credit Shaft Inventory.Customer receiptDebit Cash/Bank; Credit Accounts Receivable (for the allocated portion).Purchase on creditDebit Shaft Inventory (including landed cost); Credit Accounts Payable.Supplier paymentDebit Accounts Payable; Credit Cash/Bank.CuttingTransfer carrying value between shaft inventory and child-piece/scrap inventory; post any real unexplained loss to Cutting Loss Expense.Scrap saleDebit Cash/Receivable; Credit Scrap Sales; and separately debit Scrap Cost/COGS and credit Scrap Inventory.Operating expenseDebit the relevant expense account; Credit Cash/Bank or Accounts Payable.The final chart of accounts and exact journal posting rules should be approved before production. The examples above describe the intended accounting direction, not a substitute for accountant review.9.2 ExpensesExpenses have a category, date, amount, payment method/account, payee, reference and notes.An expense paid immediately credits cash/bank; an unpaid expense creates a payable.Confirmed expenses are corrected by reversal/adjustment, not deletion.Expense reports should support date range and category filters.10. Database integrity and transaction requirementsUse PostgreSQL through Supabase. Use UUID primary keys and owner_id on business data.Use NUMERIC/DECIMAL for PKR amounts, kg, feet, and rates; define scale and rounding policy.Use foreign keys and composite owner-scoped foreign keys where needed to prevent cross-owner references.Use CHECK constraints for positive weights/lengths, valid statuses, non-negative values where appropriate, and exactly-one-of relationships such as inventory piece vs scrap lot on a sale line.Use unique constraints for piece_code and document numbers within the appropriate business/owner scope.Use database transactions and row-level locks for purchase receiving, cutting, sales, payment allocations and reversals.Lock rows in a consistent order to reduce deadlocks and prevent concurrent overselling or negative stock.Prevent negative available stock and negative cost balances.Do not let the frontend directly edit stock balances, WAC balances, posted journal lines, or confirmed transaction history.Create stock movements and journal entries in the same transaction as the business event.Use deterministic document numbering that is safe under concurrent requests.Use immutable or append-only patterns for confirmed transactions, stock movements, journal entries and audit history.12. Security and access controlThe current application has one owner/admin user.Use Supabase Auth for sign-in; do not expose service-role credentials in the browser.Enable Row Level Security (RLS) on every owner-scoped table.Policies must restrict records to the authenticated owner_id.Sensitive writes should happen through vetted database functions/RPCs that validate ownership and business rules.Prefer SECURITY INVOKER unless a carefully reviewed SECURITY DEFINER function is genuinely required; if used, set a safe search_path and validate auth/ownership explicitly.Audit logs should be insert-only for the application user; do not allow ordinary update/delete access.Never trust totals, rates, stock quantities or owner identifiers supplied by the browser without database-side validation.13. Transaction lifecycle and reversalsOperational documents should support a draft stage where editing is allowed, followed by confirmation/posting. Once confirmed, the document becomes part of the permanent business history.Draft: can be edited or discarded according to permissions.Confirmed/posted: creates inventory movements and/or accounting journals; must not be physically deleted.Cancelled/reversed: a separate compensating record or reversal entry restores the correct balances while preserving the original event.Reversal rules must verify downstream dependencies. For example, a cut cannot be reversed if a child piece has already been sold or cut again.A reversal must reverse the associated stock movements, payment allocations and accounting journals consistently.14. Known implementation gaps to resolve before productionAn earlier SQL foundation was drafted, including core master tables, inventory/purchase/cutting structures, some views, document numbering, and initial purchase-receiving and cutting RPCs. That foundation is not yet a complete production system. The following items remain necessary and must be reviewed and tested:Complete accounting schema: chart of accounts, journal entries, journal lines, balanced-entry validation and posting functions.Complete atomic sale RPCs for full pieces, cut pieces and scrap lots, including stock consumption, WAC/COGS and revenue/receivable posting.Complete payment and allocation RPCs with partial payments, multi-invoice allocation, advances, reversals and validation.Purchase payment/supplier ledger integration and proper posting of purchase landed costs.Separate scrap valuation balance and scrap sale accounting.Cutting variance and cutting-loss journal treatment.Expense posting, stock adjustments, returns/credit notes and reversal workflows.Append-only stock movements, journal history and audit logs; tighten audit policies to prevent updates/deletes.Rework customer/supplier outstanding calculations to use allocations and adjustments rather than subtracting every receipt from total sales.Prevent overlapping effective pricing periods and define how the active rate is selected.Use consistent locking and concurrency rules for cost balances and inventory pieces.Resolve rounding so purchase and cutting cost allocations reconcile exactly.Add comprehensive RLS policies and ensure every new table/function is covered.Add automated reconciliation and tests for inventory pieces vs aggregate stock and cost balances.Review all SQL migrations for composite foreign keys, ownership validation, precision, deadlocks and negative balances.Production-readiness warning  Do not treat the earlier SQL snippets as complete or production-ready. Build and test the remaining migrations and workflows before entering real business transactions.19. Acceptance criteriaEvery received shaft is individually identifiable and has measured length and weight.The system can show current sellable stock and value for each product.WAC recalculates correctly after purchases and remains unaffected by scrap inventory.Cutting creates traceable child pieces and separately tracked scrap; source stock cannot be reused.Cutting output, scrap and variance reconcile to the source quantity/value under the defined precision policy.Full and cut sales use the correct separate rate and snapshot it on the invoice.Scrap sales use scrap-specific pricing and scrap inventory valuation.No piece or scrap quantity can be sold twice or reduced below zero.Partial payments and multi-invoice allocations calculate outstanding balances correctly.Every posted financial journal balances: total debits equal total credits.Confirmed business records are corrected through reversals, not deletion.Only the authenticated owner can access the business data.Dashboard and reports reconcile to underlying inventory, transaction and accounting records.21. Decisions still requiring owner/accountant confirmationWhether product identity is defined by diameter + grade/material + standard length, and whether any other specification fields are required.Whether scrap selling rate is maintained as a price list or entered manually on each scrap sale.The exact allocation policy for transport/handling and other landed purchase costs.The precision and rounding policy for kg, feet, PKR rates, line totals and cost allocations.Whether discounts, customer returns, supplier returns, taxes, withholding, and credit limits are required in the first release.Which cash/bank accounts and payment methods must be supported.Whether multiple warehouses, branches, or multiple owners will ever be needed.The chart of accounts and financial statement format approved by the business accountant.22.
